Key findings

Air pollution has serious consequences for Nepal

The World Bank's 2025 assessment links air pollution to about 26,000 premature deaths a year in Nepal and an average loss of 3.4 years of life expectancy. It estimates the economic cost at more than 6% of GDP annually.

These are national estimates, not figures for Kathmandu Valley alone. Kathmandu Valley and the Terai are named as Nepal's air-pollution hotspots.

World Bank press release (2025)

Fine particles (PM2.5) are important to monitor

PM2.5 particles are 2.5 micrometres across or smaller. They are small enough to reach deep into the respiratory system, and exposure is linked to adverse health outcomes. The WHO 2021 guideline levels are:

Pollution comes from many sources

The World Bank points to vehicle emissions, industry, household cooking fuels, road dust and forest fires. Pollution also crosses municipal and national borders, so local action alone may not be enough.

  • Vehicles
  • Industry

  • Road dust
  • Forest fires

The share of each source changes with place, season, weather and pollutant. This project does not assign percentages without source-specific data.

Explore the data

Open Data Nepal publishes Air Quality Data in Kathmandu, covering 1 January 2015 to 13 March 2021. It includes PM2.5, PM10, ozone, sulfur dioxide and nitrogen dioxide.

Questions this dataset can help answer:

  • How do recorded concentrations change over time?
  • Do pollutants or monitoring locations differ?
  • How do readings compare with WHO levels, when units and averaging periods match?

The data ends in March 2021, so it cannot describe current conditions. Any conclusion should name the period and locations it covers.

What could help

Method and limitations

This is a secondary-research project. It reviews published institutional research, WHO guidelines and public data. It is not an original health study or a new measurement campaign.

  1. Review the World Bank assessment for Nepal's burden, sources and responses.
  2. Use WHO guidelines as health-based reference levels.
  3. Identify public Kathmandu air-quality datasets.
  4. Keep national statistics separate from Kathmandu-specific evidence.
  5. Check the age, coverage, units and limits of data before comparing.

Limitations

  • National estimates are not Kathmandu-only statistics.
  • WHO levels are references, not local readings.
  • Historical data may not reflect today.
  • Missing observations, station differences and mixed averaging periods affect comparisons.
  • Naming a source does not show how much it contributes.

Next steps: use recent, validated station data to compare levels over time, study seasonal patterns, and test whether local readings exceed WHO levels.

Conclusion

Air pollution is a serious environmental and public-health problem in Nepal, and Kathmandu Valley is one of its hotspots. It has many sources and needs coordinated responses, not a single fix.

Breathing Kathmandu brings together credible research, open data and honest statements of uncertainty, so that reliable information can support informed discussion and practical action for cleaner air.
